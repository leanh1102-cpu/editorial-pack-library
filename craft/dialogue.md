# KX6 — Dialogue Craft

**Status:** `CANDIDATE-STABLE / VALIDATED`

## CONCEPT
Dialogue is social action performed through language under specific relations, goals, constraints, and pressure. Literary dialogue should therefore be judged by what speakers are trying to do to or with one another, not by whether their sentences share one polished register.

Core route:

`SPEAKER GOAL → ADDRESSEE → RELATION/STATUS → PRESSURE → UTTERANCE → EXCHANGE TEXTURE → IMPLICATION/WITHHOLDING → RESPONSE → RECOVERY`

## DEFINITION
- **Speaker goal:** the practical, relational, defensive, persuasive, evasive, ceremonial, or informational purpose motivating a turn.
- **Addressee model:** what the speaker assumes, fears, wants, or expects from the listener.
- **Social action:** what an utterance does in interaction beyond its literal proposition: requesting, refusing, warning, accusing, placating, testing, stalling, aligning, etc.
- **Speech condition:** situational factors such as rehearsal, ceremony, intoxication, shock, hierarchy, privacy, fatigue, or threat that can alter surface voice.
- **Exchange texture:** the patterned relation among several turns: their length, syntax, address, particles, hesitation, indirectness, overlap, silence, action support, and speaker differentiation under changing pressure.
- **Terse saturation:** an exchange-level failure in which many individually plausible short turns accumulate into proposition-only speech that no longer carries enough relational, emotional, social, or voice information for the scene. Brevity itself is not the failure.
- **Relational signal:** any supplied or context-supported feature that helps make speaker relation/status audible, including forms of address, kinship terms, particles, indirectness, self-repair, register choice, or characteristic ellipsis. No single marker is mandatory.
- **Withholding:** information or response deliberately not supplied despite communicative relevance.
- **Recovery:** return toward baseline speech after a temporary pressure- or situation-driven register shift. Recovery can include syntax expanding again after an emergency if the character and scene support it.

## SCOPE
Use this module to diagnose or craft dialogue involving indirect action, hierarchy, conflict, persuasion, evasion, differentiated voice, interruption, silence, prepared speech, state-dependent register change, or exchange-level texture.

Do not use it to:
- normalize all characters toward one literary standard;
- infer hidden intention as fact when the scene only supports a plausible reading;
- convert direct thought into spoken dialogue merely for liveliness;
- force every character to display a fixed surface voice in every situation;
- correct stable dialect/idiolect solely because it departs from prestige usage;
- impose a minimum or maximum turn length;
- lengthen every short line merely to avoid terseness.

## MECHANISM
### 1. Identify the social task of the turn
Ask what the speaker is attempting: obtain action, protect face, threaten, conceal, recruit, deflect, confess, delay, or maintain ritual/social order.

### 2. Map speaker–addressee relation
Status, intimacy, obligation, conflict history, role, and audience affect what can be said directly and what is safer to imply.

### 3. Separate literal content from plausible force
A declarative can plausibly function as a request or warning. Preserve the distinction between what was explicitly said and what the listener/reader may infer.

### 4. Track pressure and speech condition
Voice is stateful. A rough speaker may deliver rehearsed ceremonial language; a formal speaker may briefly drop register under surprise and recover later.

### 5. Read silence, interruption, and withholding as actions only when context supports them
Silence may refuse, conceal, concede, punish, process, or simply mark uncertainty. Do not assign one meaning automatically.

### 6. Preserve differentiated voices
Variation can follow region, education, role, relation, preparation, immediate state, and social purpose. Consistency means behavioral logic, not identical syntax across all contexts.

### 7. Audit the exchange, not only isolated turns
After individual turns make sense, read several adjacent turns as one interactional unit.

Ask:
- Are many turns reduced to bare propositions even though pressure does not require compression?
- Is relation/status still audible, or has all address and social shading disappeared?
- Do different speakers converge on the same clipped syntax?
- Is surrounding action carrying the emotional/contextual load, or is the dialogue expected to carry it alone?
- When acute pressure falls, does speech have a plausible chance to recover toward each speaker's baseline?

A short turn can be excellent. A sequence of short turns can also be excellent. Repair only when **accumulation** creates a material loss of relation, voice, context, or readability.

### 8. Repair terse saturation with the smallest useful intervention
Do not pad every line. Change only enough of the exchange to restore functional variation. Depending on evidence, this may mean:
- keeping the sharpest short turns at pressure peaks;
- allowing one or two turns to expand when a speaker must persuade, soften, evade, reassure, justify, or recover;
- restoring established forms of address or relational markers;
- preserving hesitation/self-repair when they carry social pressure;
- giving speakers different response shapes rather than different decorative catchphrases;
- using surrounding action instead of explanatory dialogue when action already bears the emotion.

## TEXTUAL SIGNALS
- indirect declaratives near actionable situations;
- abrupt refusals or repeated warnings;
- honorific/address differences;
- clipped versus elaborated turns;
- long runs of similarly clipped proposition-only turns;
- several distinct speakers converging on the same terse syntax;
- emergency compression that persists unchanged after pressure falls;
- disappearance of previously established forms of address or relational shading;
- interruption, overlap, self-repair, hesitation;
- stable regional or idiolectal forms;
- temporary ceremonial/formal speech;
- register collapse under surprise or threat followed by recovery;
- topic shifts used to evade or control;
- unreturned questions or strategic silence.

## EFFECTS
Dialogue choices can create:
- hierarchy and resistance;
- intimacy or distance;
- conflict and negotiation;
- indirect pressure;
- character differentiation;
- concealment and reader inference;
- comic or tragic mismatch between literal and social meaning;
- state change visible through register shift;
- pressure-sensitive rhythm across an exchange.

Effects require interactional evidence; one lexical marker or one short sentence alone does not prove a complete social role, psychology, or texture failure.

## CONDITIONS
Preserve dialogue variation when:
- the speaker/addressee relation supports it;
- regional/idiolectal features are stable and intelligible enough for the task;
- register shift matches rehearsal, ceremony, shock, threat, or another established condition;
- repetition/interruption changes pressure or interactional position;
- indirectness remains inferable without redundant explanation;
- clipped turns occur at genuine pressure peaks or surrounding action carries what speech omits;
- mixed turn lengths follow different social tasks rather than a cosmetic pattern.

Repair when:
- the turn cannot plausibly serve the intended social action;
- voice changes without any established condition and breaks behavioral logic;
- attribution/reference makes speaker identity unclear materially;
- exposition is placed in dialogue without a plausible addressee reason;
- dialect markers become random decoration rather than stable voice behavior;
- many adjacent turns remain bare and interchangeable despite moderate/low pressure and insufficient contextual support;
- distinct speakers collapse into one generic terse register;
- acute-pressure compression continues after a clear pressure drop with no character-specific reason;
- proposition-only compression removes material relation/status/context that the scene needs the dialogue to carry.

## COUNTEREXAMPLES
- `Ở đây gió lùa quá.` may function as an indirect request, but the request is not explicit fact.
- A district clerk and dock worker can legitimately use different syntax/register in the same exchange.
- A rough speaker can produce smoother language in a rehearsed memorial oath and then return to baseline.
- `Đừng. Đừng mở. Đừng mở cái cửa đó.` can be functional escalation rather than redundant repetition.
- Stable `bển`, `má`, `hổng` in one character's dialogue need not be standardized when intelligible and contextually grounded.
- `Cha.` / `Ngủ tiếp đi.` can remain short when surrounding body/action already carries the relational and emotional load.
- A calm family exchange made almost entirely of `Biết.` / `Không.` / `Mai.` / `Tính.` may need revision if no surrounding action or established voice supplies what the clipped syntax removes.
- Direct interior thought remains thought unless the scene establishes actual utterance.

## COMMON ERRORS
- same-voice smoothing;
- **AI terse register:** many speakers defaulting to the same clipped proposition-only syntax;
- treating every short turn as automatically powerful, restrained, or literary;
- treating exchange-level saturation as if each sentence could be judged independently;
- solving terse saturation by lengthening every turn;
- prestige normalization of dialect/idiolect;
- deleting forms of address, particles, hesitation, or self-repair solely as filler when they are carrying relation/pressure;
- treating prepared speech as voice inconsistency;
- making every indirect intention explicit;
- explaining silence inside narration before the scene supplies evidence;
- using one catchphrase as the entire character voice;
- adding filler merely to make dialogue sound natural;
- turning thought into speech for pace;
- treating visible emotion as identical to inner emotion.

## CLOSE READING METHOD
For each important exchange, map:

`WHO SPEAKS → TO WHOM → WHAT IS SAID → WHAT ACTION IS PLAUSIBLE → STATUS/RELATION/PRESSURE → EXCHANGE TEXTURE → WHAT IS WITHHELD → RESPONSE → PRESSURE CHANGE → RECOVERY`

Keep explicit wording separate from inferred social action. Judge both the local turn and the accumulated effect of adjacent turns.

## CRAFT TRANSFER
Transfer interactional mechanics rather than phrases.

Examples:
- recreate an indirect request using a different setting, relation, and literal statement;
- recreate escalating refusal with different wording and action thresholds;
- recreate prepared/baseline contrast using a different ceremony and character profile;
- recreate emergency compression followed by a believable recovery segment without making all later turns long;
- rewrite a proposition-only family exchange so relation becomes audible while keeping at least some short turns.

## DRILLS
1. Label ten dialogue turns by literal content and plausible social action separately.
2. Rewrite one exchange to preserve status difference without caricature.
3. Build a three-turn escalation where repetition changes pressure each time.
4. Write a prepared speech that differs from baseline voice but remains behaviorally plausible.
5. Diagnose three silences without assigning intention unless the surrounding evidence supports it.
6. Compare two eight-turn exchanges: one functionally clipped under pressure and one terse-saturated at low pressure; explain why only the second needs repair.
7. Repair a terse-saturated exchange without increasing every turn's length.

## AI OPERATIONAL RULES
- Dialogue is judged through speaker goal, addressee, relation, status, pressure, social purpose, **and exchange-level texture**.
- Explicit content and implicature remain distinct.
- Preserve dialect/idiolect when stable, intelligible, and character-bound unless a task-specific readability problem is demonstrated.
- Do not enforce one surface voice across preparation, shock, ceremony, fatigue, intimacy, or recovery states.
- Preserve functional interruption, roughness, repetition, short turns, and silence when their interactional work is demonstrated.
- **Do not infer quality from brevity.** One short turn or many short turns are not automatically good or bad.
- Detect terse saturation only from accumulation plus material loss: flattened relation, lost speaker differentiation, missing contextual/emotional shading, or implausible lack of recovery.
- When repairing terse saturation, preserve the strongest functional short turns and change the smallest number of surrounding turns needed to restore variation.
- Do not create a word-count quota for dialogue turns.
- Do not treat established forms of address, particles, hesitation, self-repair, or indirectness as filler by default; preserve them when they carry relation, register, state, or social purpose.
- Do not manufacture particles, dialect markers, or kinship terms unsupported by the supplied voice/context merely to make dialogue sound Vietnamese.
- Do not infer deception, motive, or inner emotion from one turn alone.
- Do not convert direct thought into spoken dialogue without evidence that it is uttered.

## SELF-CHECK
- What does the speaker want from this addressee now?
- What is explicit, and what am I inferring?
- How do relation/status/pressure shape the turn?
- What does the **whole adjacent exchange** sound like, not just this sentence?
- Are several speakers collapsing into one generic terse register?
- If turns are clipped, what carries the omitted context: pressure, action, established voice, or nothing?
- Did pressure change? If so, did speech have a plausible chance to recover?
- Did I preserve relational/register markers that are doing real work?
- Did I lengthen dialogue merely to satisfy an imagined naturalness quota?
- Is the voice difference stable or condition-driven?
- Did I normalize dialect or roughness merely for polish?
- Does repetition/interruption change the interaction?
- Did I assign a meaning to silence without evidence?
- Did I preserve thought/speech boundaries?
